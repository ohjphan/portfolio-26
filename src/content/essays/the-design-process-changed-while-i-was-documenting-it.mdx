Less than a year ago, I was trying to define the design process for our team at CZI Learning Commons.

I mapped out the phases, the purpose of each one, the expected outputs, and who should be involved. The goal was practical: help people know when to collaborate, what kind of feedback would be useful, and how to move through the work faster.

## Why I needed a map

There was a personal reason I wanted to do this, too. Most of my career had been spent at startups, where I was used to wearing many hats and moving with a lot of autonomy. CZI was the first larger organization I’d worked in where people had more clearly defined roles and responsibilities. I sometimes found myself crossing boundaries without realizing it, because I was working the way I always had. I could see that my pace and process weren’t always easy for others to work with.

Mapping the process was partly my way of learning how to work better in this environment. I wanted to make my own approach more visible, understand where others needed to be involved, and give us a shared way to move quickly together.

## The process I drew

The framework followed four broad phases: Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver. Underneath them, the work moved in a familiar sequence:

![Napkin sketch of the design process: Research, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, Refine, Build, QA, Launch](/images/writing/design-process-linear-sketch.png)

I still believe that framework served a purpose. When a team is unclear about ownership, expectations, or what “ready” means, a shared process creates clarity. But looking at it today, I’m struck by how quickly the shape of the work has changed.

## How AI reshaped the loop

With AI, I can take an idea and build something I can actually use much earlier. I can sketch a flow in the morning, have something clickable by the afternoon, put it in front of a teammate, find the awkward parts, and change it before the day is over. Using the same language, the loop often looks more like:

![Napkin sketch of the AI-reshaped loop: Define, Build and Test in a circular loop, Refine, QA, Launch](/images/writing/design-process-ai-loop-sketch.png)

Research hasn’t vanished. Neither have design, collaboration, or careful thinking. They happen throughout the loop, often in response to something tangible. A working version can reveal questions that a spec or static mockup might never have surfaced.

## Building sooner isn’t skipping the hard questions

And building sooner doesn’t mean we should launch without scrutiny. We still need to ask whether we’re solving the right problem, whether people can use what we’ve made, and whether it works beyond the happy path. AI makes it easier to produce an answer. It doesn’t tell us whether that answer is any good.

What has changed is the cost of learning. We can get to a real experience faster, which gives us a chance to learn from the experience itself. A prototype may now be functional enough to test the product idea, the interaction, and the technical constraints at the same time. Design and build can inform each other continuously.

## What a good process is for

I find that exciting. I also find it a little humbling. I put real thought into documenting a process that, less than a year later, I would draw differently. But the act of making it helped me understand something I needed to learn at the time: moving fast as an individual is different from helping a team move well together.

Maybe that’s what a good process is supposed to do. It gives a team a useful way to work for now. It shouldn’t become a set of steps we defend long after the tools, the team, and the work have changed.

The point was never to complete every phase in the right order. The point was to make better decisions together and get something valuable into people’s hands.

That part hasn’t changed. The way we get there will keep evolving, and we should be willing to evolve with it.
