The other night, after the second book and before the lights went out, my daughter asked why some evenings feel longer than others even when the clock says the same thing.

I didn’t have a clean answer. I said something about tiredness and how time stretches when you’re waiting. She nodded like that was enough, rolled onto her side, and was gone within a minute.

I stayed in the doorway longer than I needed to.

I’ve noticed something about the people whose work I admire most: they never stopped asking questions like that. Not the performative kind — the kind that sounds smart in a meeting, polished for an audience, already halfway to a slide. The quiet kind. The ones that show up while you’re stirring something on the stove, walking the block after dinner, or sitting on the edge of a bed in the dark.

Questions that don’t have immediate answers and aren’t meant to.

## The compound effect

Curiosity compounds the same way interest does, but slower and deeper.

One question leads to a book. The book leads to a conversation. The conversation leads to an experiment. None of it was planned. It was just followed.

Years ago I kept circling a soft question about craft — why making something with my hands felt different from shipping software, even when both required care. That wondering eventually became Hathorway, my jewelry company. I didn’t start with a business plan. I started with a pull toward traditional Vietnamese craft and a stubborn curiosity about whether I could make physical things that felt as considered as the products I’d spent years designing on screens.

The leap looked sudden from the outside. From the inside, it was just follow-through on a question I’d been carrying around for a long time.

I keep a running list of things I’m wondering about. Not a to-do list — a wonder list. Right now it includes:

- Why do some rooms make me want to create and others make me want to leave before I’ve hung up my coat?
- What would learning look like if we designed for curiosity instead of compliance?
- Why do the best conversations still happen in kitchens, standing up, with something unfinished on the counter?
- What happens when you leave enough empty space in a week for a ridiculous idea to show up — and enough energy to follow it?

None of these have deadlines. That’s the point.

## Making room

The hardest part isn’t finding interesting questions. It’s making room for them in a life that rewards answers.

We optimize for output. For shipping. For closing loops. For being the person who already knows.

But the most valuable creative work often starts in an open loop — a question you can’t stop turning over, even when you have no idea where it leads. Leaving room for that used to feel inefficient. Now it feels like the only way anything unexpected arrives.

That’s where the good stuff lives. Not in the certainty. In the wondering.

Tonight I’ll probably put the kids to bed again, stand in the kitchen for a minute longer than necessary, and add one more soft question to the list — something I won’t answer before morning, and maybe not for years.
