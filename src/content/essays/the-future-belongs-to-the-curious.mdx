The future belongs to the curious.

It probably always has, and it probably always will.

Lately, I’ve been spending a lot of time thinking about values. Not in an abstract way — in a very personal one. How I want to live. How I want to show up. And how I want to pass those values on to my two beautiful children.

There are four I try to practice every day:

Stay curious.

Have grit.

Be kind.

Show gratitude.

It took me months to hone in on this short list. Though in reality, it took me over thirty years to arrive here.

Today, I want to talk about the first. It’s first for a reason.

Everything else seems to grow from it.

## Stay curious

I didn’t grow up with a clear roadmap to success. I didn’t have early access to the kinds of opportunities that quietly compound over time.

My mother was an immigrant from a small village in Vietnam and, for many years, a single mother. She worked hard and learned how to navigate life in a new country. But she didn’t know the invisible rules that shape education, careers, and opportunity here.

She believed that as long as I stayed in school, I would be okay.

But in school, I struggled.

So I had to find another way.

I stayed curious.

I read constantly. I watched how people moved through the world. I asked questions. I experimented. I tried things before I felt ready.

Curiosity became my education.

Over time, I learned that if you stay curious long enough, you can teach yourself almost anything. You can build without permission. You can create paths where none seem to exist.

I learned that curiosity can outsmart “smarts.”

Curiosity doesn’t just open doors.

It teaches you how to build them.

## Curiosity in the age of AI

We are in an era where AI can do an extraordinary amount of execution.

You don’t need to know how to code to build. You don’t need years of experience to prototype. You don’t need a perfect plan to begin.

You just need curiosity.

Tools like Cursor, Figma Make, and others are collapsing the distance between imagination and reality.

This will blur roles. It will flatten credentials. It will make traditional markers of success feel increasingly irrelevant.

AI has the “how” covered. Just about anyone can build anything in days, overnight, or even in minutes.

What it won’t cover is the “why.”

It won’t tell you which questions to ask. It won’t know what is worth building. It won’t know what should exist in the first place.

That still belongs to us.

And as a parent, that realization hits differently.

## What I hope my children learn

When I think about the world my children will grow up in, I don’t worry about whether they will learn the “right” skills or choose the “right” career path.

I bet most of those skills don’t exist yet. And many of today’s roles won’t either.

What I hope they learn is how to wonder. How to ask questions. How to explore without fear of being wrong. How to stay open when the answers aren’t obvious.

In a world where anyone can generate output, the most valuable people will not be the ones with the most answers.

They will be the ones with the most interesting questions.

Curiosity is what keeps you relevant. It’s what keeps you adaptable.

It’s what keeps you human.

## The future belongs to the curious

This is something I’ve always looked for when hiring. It’s what I look for in the people I choose to work with and for. And now, it’s something I try to nurture at home.

Because the truth is: skills age quickly. Tools update constantly.

Curiosity compounds.

Steve Jobs said it best: “Stay hungry. Stay foolish.”

Stay hungry enough to keep learning. Stay foolish enough to try before you are ready. Stay open enough to be wrong, to change your mind, to begin again.

Because curiosity is a survival skill.

And if there’s one thing I hope to pass on to my children — long before the world teaches them to play it safe — it’s this:

Stay curious.

Because as the future continues to unfold faster than we can predict, curiosity will not just help them adapt.

It will help them lead.
