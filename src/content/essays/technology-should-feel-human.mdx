Last week my kid asked for help with a homework question, and before I could reach for a pencil, a tab had already opened with an answer waiting — polished, confident, and completely empty of how she was thinking.

Something in me flinched.

Not because the answer was wrong. Because the posture was wrong. It arrived like a finished thought, when what she needed was a companion for the messy middle.

Every new technology arrives with a promise and a posture. Some announce themselves loudly. Others slip into daily life so naturally you forget they weren’t always there.

AI is different. It’s arriving everywhere at once — in our writing tools, our search bars, our children’s homework, our creative workflows. And we’re still figuring out what posture it should take.

## The human test

When I evaluate a new AI tool, I don’t ask how powerful it is. I ask whether it feels human.

Human doesn’t mean pretending to be a person. It means:

- Respecting my attention instead of demanding it
- Understanding context instead of starting from zero every time
- Knowing when to help and when to step back
- Leaving room for my thinking, not replacing it

I’ve used AI that feels like a thoughtful collaborator — someone who listens, offers a perspective, and then gets out of the way. I’ve also used AI that feels like a coworker who talks over you in meetings: fast, impressive, and somehow leaving you with less of your own voice than you started with.

The difference isn’t usually the model. It’s the design around it. What it assumes you want. What it rushes to complete. What it leaves unfinished on purpose.

The best experiences don’t replace the work of thinking. They sit beside it.

## Care, not spectacle

How we design AI is how we teach it to behave in the world.

Every interface decision is a small act of care — or the absence of it. Do we default to generating, or to asking? Do we show our work, or hide it behind confidence? Do we optimize for speed, or for understanding?

These aren’t only technical questions. They’re design questions. And design, at its best, is how we decide what kind of company we want technology to be when someone is tired, curious, hurried, or six years old and trying to figure something out before dinner.

I don’t need AI that impresses me in a demo. I need AI that remembers I’m still in the room — that my attention is finite, my context matters, and sometimes the kindest thing software can do is wait.

The technology will keep getting more capable. The question that matters is whether it gets more human along the way.

Tonight, when homework comes out again, I’ll sit next to her with a blank page between us. If we open a tool, I want it to ask what she’s stuck on — not hand her a paragraph that skips the part where she learns.
